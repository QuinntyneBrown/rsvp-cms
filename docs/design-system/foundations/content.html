Content

Overview

Invitely's words sit between a host and the people they love, so they sound like a thoughtful friend who is good with spreadsheets: warm, plain and exact. Organisers need to know precisely what will happen and when (“Sends at 9:00 am in the event time zone.”); guests need to answer in a few seconds and feel welcome doing it (“Joyfully accepting”).

  • Plain: everyday words, short sentences, one idea each.
  • Exact: real names, real numbers, real dates. “Remove 2 guests?”, never “Remove selected items?”.
  • Warm, not cute: the occasion carries the joy; the interface stays calm, especially when something fails.
  • Reassuring: after a failure, say what is safe (“Nothing was changed.”) before what to do.

Grammar and mechanics

  • Sentence case for everything: titles, buttons, labels, tabs, badges in source text (badges render in capitals with CSS; write them as “Invites open”).
  • British English: organiser, recognise, cancelled. The product is used by hosts in the UK and beyond; the spelling is consistent with the mocks.
  • Contractions are welcome: “can’t”, “didn’t”, “you’re”. Use typographic apostrophes and quotes (’ “ ”), not straight ones.
  • No full stop on titles, buttons, labels, tabs, badges or toast and alert titles. Full stops on help text, body text and error messages, even single sentences.
  • Ellipsis (…, one character) only for progress (“Sending your RSVP…”) and truncated text, never on a button that opens a dialog.
  • Second person for the reader (“your events”); first person plural for Invitely when it failed (“We couldn’t load your guests”).
  • Numbers as numerals everywhere, including one to nine: “2 guests”, “3 rows were skipped.”

Error messages

Field errors follow one of two formulas; both are full sentences ending in a full stop.

Something is missing

Enter / Choose+the thing+an example (optional)

  • “Enter an email like name@example.com.”
  • “Choose whether you’re coming.”
  • “Enter a headline guests will read first.”

Something is wrong

What is wrong, plainly+How to fix it

  • “That date has already passed. Pick a date after today.”
  • “Invites must close after they open. Choose a later close date.”
  • “The name doesn’t match. Type ‘Maya’s 30th birthday’ exactly.”
  • “Email is already used by Sam Whitfield in this event.”

Something failed on our side

We couldn’t + verb + thing+What is safe+What to do

  • “We couldn’t load your guests.” “Nothing was changed. Try again to see the list.”
  • “Your RSVP didn’t save.” “Your answer is still on this page. Check your connection and try again.”
  • “Something went wrong on our side.” “We’ve logged the problem… Reference: INV-7C41.” (500 pages carry a reference.)
  • Never blame the reader (“You entered an invalid email”) and never use “invalid”, “error” or codes as the whole message.
  • The error summary title counts: “2 things to fix”.

Confirmations and success

  • Dialog title is a question naming the thing: “Delete ‘Maya’s 30th birthday’?”, “Remove 2 guests?”, “Discard unpublished changes?”, “Reset invitation links?”.
  • Body states the consequence with numbers: “This removes the page, 84 invitees and 52 responses.”
  • Confirm button repeats the verb and object (“Delete event”, “Remove 2 guests”, “Reset links”); the safe button says what is kept (“Keep editing”, “Keep my reply”) or “Cancel”.
  • Success titles state the outcome in past tense without a full stop (“Event details saved”, “RSVP saved”); the text adds the next step or detail (“A confirmation email is on its way.”).
  • Partial success says both halves: “Saved, but the email didn’t send”.

Usage

Accessibility

  • Write link and button text that makes sense out of context (WCAG 2.4.4): “View public page”, not “View”.
  • Icon-only buttons need the same care in their aria-label: verb plus object (“Remove guest Jonah Adeyemi”).
  • Labels are visible and say what to enter; placeholders show an example at most.
  • Error text names the field and the fix (3.3.1, 3.3.3); the summary title counts the problems.
  • Plain language helps everyone, including screen-reader users hearing it once: front-load the important word (“RSVP closes tomorrow at 11:59 pm.”).
  • Do not rely on capitals for meaning; badge text is written in sentence case and transformed by CSS, so screen readers read words, not letters.

Tokens

Content has no tokens. The type that carries it (--text-body, --text-label, --text-caption, --text-overline and the heading roles) is documented on the typography page, and the measure that caps line length (--layout-measure, 65ch) on the layout page.
